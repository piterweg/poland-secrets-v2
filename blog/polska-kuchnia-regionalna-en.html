Polish Regional Cuisine — 10 Dishes You Must Try

Kibiny, oscypek, kartacze, roulade — Polish cuisine is not just pierogi and pork chops. It is a dozen different regional cuisines, each with its own history. A guide to 10 dishes, from the Baltic to the Bieszczady.

Polish cuisine is unfairly reduced to pierogi, pork chops, and bigos. Meanwhile, it is a dozen different regional cuisines, each with its own traditions, ingredients, and flavours. Poland lies at the crossroads of trade routes — from the Baltic to the Carpathians, from Germany to Ruthenia — and each of these encounters left a trace on the plate. Kashubian cuisine smells of smokehouse smoke, Silesian — of the heavy sauce of roulade, Podlasie — of Tatar pepper, Podhale — of sheep cheese. This guide shows 10 dishes you must try at least once in your life, and links to detailed articles about each regional cuisine.

Note for travellers: regional cuisine tastes only where it is part of everyday life — in a small inn, milk bar, agritourism farm. Not in a restaurant on a tourist market square. All recommended places in this guide are establishments where the cuisine is home-style, not “for show”.

Northern Cuisine — Baltic and Lakes

The north of Poland is a land of fish — Baltic and lake fish. Pomeranian cuisine is dominated by herring, flounder, eel, and smoked fish. Kashubian — by golce, herring, and eel from the lakes. Warmian-Masurian — by vendace, carp, and fish from Masurian lakes. It is a simple cuisine, based on what the sea and lakes provided — with a clear influence of German and Scandinavian cuisine, because Pomerania belonged for centuries to the Teutonic state, then to Prussia.

See details: Pomeranian cuisine — fish, herring, golce, szneki z glancem. Kashubian cuisine — herring, eel, Kashubian broth, golce. Kuyavian cuisine — Toruń gingerbread, żur, kołocz (Kuyavia is the borderland of Pomerania and Greater Poland).

1. Oscypek (Podhale)

Smoked sheep's cheese, formed into characteristic spindles. Served grilled, with cranberry, straight from the shepherd. The taste of the mountains that cannot be faked. Inscribed on the EU protected products list — real oscypek can only be bought in Podhale. Details in the article Podhale cuisine.

2. Kwaśnica (Podhale)

Highland soup based on sauerkraut with meat — usually ribs or bacon. It warms from the first sip. Perfect after a day in the mountains. It is eaten mainly in autumn and winter, especially after returning from a trail. Details in the article Podhale cuisine.

Eastern Cuisine — Borderland of Cultures

The east of Poland is borderland cuisine — Polish, Ukrainian, Lemko, Boyko, Tatar, and Jewish traditions meet on one plate. It is a simple, rural cuisine, based on groats, potatoes, cabbage, and mushrooms. The Białowieża Forest provides mushrooms, Podlasie — Tatar kibiny, Subcarpathia — Lemko fuczki and proziaki. All these are deeply filling, cheap, and rooted in tradition.

See details: Podlasie cuisine — kibiny, kartacze, potato babka, sękacz. Subcarpathian cuisine — proziaki, fuczki, hreczanyki, San trout. Lublin cuisine — cebularze, pierogi with bryndza, Roztocze trout.

3. Kibiny (Podlasie)

Tatar meat pastries, baked or fried. A classic of Podlasie cuisine that survived for centuries — the Tatars settled in Podlasie as early as the 14th century. Thin dough, meat filling with onion. Best at milk bars in Białystok and in Kruszyniany, where a Tatar community still lives. Details in the article Podlasie cuisine.

4. Kartacze (Podlasie)

Large potato dumplings stuffed with meat. Hearty, substantial, rural. A dish that appears at every wedding in the region. Kartacze differ from ordinary dumplings in that they are much larger — even 8–10 cm — and have a more “meaty” character. Details in the article Podlasie cuisine.

5. Lublin Cebularz (Lublin Region)

Flat yeast dough with onion and poppy seeds. Sold at markets and bakeries throughout the region. Best for breakfast, freshly baked, with butter. Inscribed on the EU protected products list since 2014. Details in the article Lublin cuisine.

6. Pierogi with Bryndza (Roztocze and Lublin Region)

Delicate pierogi with sheep cheese — a specialty of the Polish-Ukrainian borderland. Served with cracklings or sour cream. Bryndza gave them a characteristic, slightly salty flavour. Today it is one of the most recognizable dishes of the Lublin region and Subcarpathia. Details in the articles Lublin cuisine and Subcarpathian cuisine.

7. Proziaki (Lemko Region and Subcarpathia)

The simplest of Lemko flatbreads — made of flour, sour milk, and baking soda, baked on a sheet. Served with garlic butter, lard, or curd cheese. Simple, cheap, deeply rural — for centuries they were the basis of the diet of poorer rural families. Details in the article Subcarpathian cuisine.

Southern Cuisine — Mountains and Silesia

The south of Poland is highlander and Silesian cuisine. In Podhale — oscypki, kwaśnica, moskole, lamb. In Lesser Poland — obwarzanki, żurek, makowiec, Kraków sausage. In Silesia — roulade, Silesian dumplings, modra kapusta, kołocz. It is a hearty cuisine, based on meat, potatoes, and cabbage, with clear German (Silesia) and Vlach (Podhale) influences.

See details: Podhale cuisine — oscypek, kwaśnica, bryjka, moskole. Lesser Poland cuisine — obwarzanek, żurek, makowiec, Kraków sausage. Silesian cuisine — roulade, Silesian dumplings, modra kapusta, kołocz. What to eat in Kraków — 10 dishes of the capital of Lesser Poland with specific places.

8. Silesian Roulade (Silesia)

Beef rolled with bacon, onion, and cucumber, served with Silesian dumplings and modra kapusta. A classic of working-class cuisine. Roulade is a Sunday dish — labour-intensive, requires patience, but the result is worth every minute. Details in the article Silesian cuisine.

Central and Western Cuisine — Heart of Poland

The centre and west of Poland is urban and rural cuisine at the same time. In Warsaw and Masovia — flaki, Jewish-style carp, poultry liver, duck. In Greater Poland — pyry z gzikiem, St Martin's croissants, duck with apples. In Lower Silesia — Silesian Heaven, Wrocław duck, dumplings, krupnioki. In Kuyavia and Pomerania — Toruń gingerbread, żur, kołocz. It is a cuisine where Polish, German, Czech, and Jewish influences meet.

See details: Masovian cuisine — flaki, krupnik, liver, Jewish-style carp. Lower Silesian cuisine — Silesian Heaven, Wrocław duck, dumplings, makiełki. Kuyavian cuisine — Toruń gingerbread, żur, kołocz, carp.

9. St Martin's Croissant (Greater Poland)

Sweet croissant with white poppy seeds, dried fruit, and almonds. Baked only on 11 November — St Martin's Day. Protected by the EU as a regional product with a protected designation of origin. The original croissant has a certificate — buy only in certified pastry shops. Details in the article Kuyavian cuisine (Poznań and Greater Poland border Kuyavia, the regions interpenetrate).

10. Smoked Vendace (Warmia-Masuria)

Delicate, fatty fish from Masurian lakes. Traditionally smoked in home smokehouses. Tastes completely different from the store version — the meat is more delicate, more aromatic, less salty. Vendace is one of the symbols of Masurian cuisine, alongside carp and eel. Details in the article Pomeranian cuisine (smoked fish, though vendace is a Masurian specialty).

How to Eat Them? Practical Tips

Regional cuisine is best eaten in the place it comes from. In a small inn, milk bar, agritourism farm. Regional cuisine tastes only where it is part of everyday life, not a tourist attraction. Here are a few rules worth remembering:

  • Avoid restaurants on main market squares — they are more expensive and touristy. Go 2 streets further — to milk bars and taverns — where the food is cheaper and better.
  • Buy directly from producers — oscypki in shepherd's huts, fish in smokehouses, bread at markets, cheeses from farmers. The price is higher, but the quality is incomparable.
  • Ask local residents for recommended places — the best milk bars and taverns are not in guidebooks.
  • Order seasonal dishes — cabbage with mushrooms in autumn, vendace in winter, eel in summer. Regional cuisine is seasonal, and that is part of its charm.
  • Don't try to eat everything in one weekend — regional cuisine is not a checklist, it is a way of travelling. Choose 2–3 dishes per trip.
  • Take something home — oscypki, honey, cheeses, gingerbread. Regional cuisine is also a souvenir that tastes better than a fridge magnet.

Summary

Polish regional cuisine is a dozen different cuisines, each with its own history and its own flavours. From herring in Pomerania, through kibiny in Podlasie, to roulade in Silesia — every region has something you will not find anywhere else. Plan your trip around Poland to try at least a few regional dishes — preferably in places where the cuisine is still home-style, not touristy.
